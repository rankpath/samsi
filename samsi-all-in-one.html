2026–2027 Colour Forecast — working reference

All hex values below are approximations for screen. Coloro codes are physical colour references, not sRGB, so treat these as starting points and adjust for contrast rather than quoting them as official values.

Contents

  1. The two anchors
  2. WGSN x Coloro S/S 27 key colours
  3. WGSN x Coloro A/W 27–28 key colours
  4. What this means on screen
  5. Digital-native trends not in the fashion forecast
  6. Sources

1. The two anchors

Pantone Colour of the Year 2026 — Cloud Dancer (PANTONE 11-4201) ≈ #F0EEE9. The first white ever chosen. Pantone frames it as a blank canvas and a "whisper of calm in a noisy world" — explicitly a response to overstimulation, and explicitly linked to the open frontier of AI. Not cool, not warm: balanced.

For web this matters more than any accent trend. It legitimises the shift already underway from #FFFFFF to soft mineral off-whites as the default canvas.

WGSN x Coloro Colour of the Year 2027 — Luminous Blue (Coloro 125-28-38) ≈ #1E3FCB. A vivid cobalt, close to lapis lazuli. WGSN's strategist describes it as "the bridge between nature and technology" and a gender-inclusive, transseasonal shade that works across fashion and consumer tech surfaces. Announced April 2025, so by 2027 it is a mature trend, not a risky bet.

The pairing is the whole story: a quiet neutral canvas carrying one confident, saturated signal colour. That is also, conveniently, the structure of a good interface.

4. What this means on screen

Fashion forecasts don't transfer directly. Translation rules:

  • A forecast colour at full saturation is almost never body text. Luminous Blue on Cloud Dancer is roughly 7:1 — fine. Maize on white is around 1.9:1 — decorative only.
  • Trend colours belong on 5–10% of the screen. The trend is the accent and the neutral, not the whole palette.
  • Warm vs cool neutral is the fastest way to date a design. Cool grey (#F5F6F8) reads 2019 SaaS. Mineral off-white (#F0EEE9, #F8F5F1) reads 2026–27.
  • Tint, don't invent. Need a soft background for a section? Use the accent at 6–10% opacity over the neutral rather than adding a new hue.

5. Digital-native trends the fashion forecast misses

These come from UI practice rather than colour forecasting, and matter more for a website than the runway colours do:

  1. Elevated neutrals. Soft grey, warm sand, stone, oatmeal, muted clay replacing stark white interfaces. Reported to reduce screen fatigue and make products feel human rather than institutional.
  2. Soft gradients 2.0. Light passing through glass, not rainbow mesh. Low chroma range, blurred edges, used on hero surfaces only.
  3. Mood mode. Dark mode as a designed palette with its own tokens, rather than an algorithmic inversion. Often a hued dark surface plus a lifted accent.
  4. Neon as micro-accent. Tiny doses on dark surfaces in developer and AI-tooling products. A 2px focus ring, a status dot — not a background.
  5. Polished brutalism. High-contrast borders and heavy type survive, but the deliberately ugly colour clashes have been replaced by disciplined complementary pairs.
  6. Adaptive / context-aware colour. Palettes that shift with time of day, content, or user state. Only worth building if the token layer is clean.
  7. Accessibility as a design constraint, not a retrofit. Focus states, non-colour status cues, and contrast budgets decided at palette time.

Palettes by website category

Eighteen ready systems, each tuned to the 2026–27 direction: a mineral neutral canvas, ink with real weight, and one accent that carries the conversion.

Every contrast ratio below was computed, not estimated. Where an accent is too light for white text, the button text colour is specified — that is the fix, not a different accent.

How to read each row: surface / ink / ink-muted / accent / accent-quiet. accent-quiet is the accent at roughly 10% over the surface — use it for selected rows, badges, and section bands.

1. B2B SaaS

#F7F6F3 / #12151C / #5B6072 / #1E3FCB / #E4E7F7 Ink 16.9:1 · muted 5.8:1 · white on accent 8.0:1. Luminous Blue does the job navy used to, with more energy. Warm-grey surface stops it feeling like every other dashboard. Accent on CTAs and active nav only.

2. AI products and developer tools

#101317 / #E9EBE6 / #8C9299 / #B8E62E / #1C2418 Ink 15.5:1 · muted 5.9:1 · dark text #101317 on the lime, 12.8:1. Dark-first with a single acid accent. Never white text on lime. Keep the lime to focus rings, active states, and one CTA per screen.

3. Healthcare and medical clinics

#F6F4F0 / #16211E / #5A6663 / #1B6B63 / #DCEAE7 Ink 15.1:1 · muted 5.4:1 · white on accent 6.3:1. Deep teal reads clinical-but-human and avoids the "spa" trap of pale mint. Older patients need the 16:1 ink — don't soften body text to grey.

4. Aesthetic and cosmetic surgery clinics

#F7F2EE / #231A18 / #6B5B54 / #8B4A33 / #EEE1DA Ink 15.3:1 · muted 5.8:1 · white on accent 6.7:1. Russet instead of gold. Gold gradients signal "luxury claim", which is exactly what advertising regulators scrutinise. Warm, expensive, quiet.

5. E-commerce and retail

#FBF8F4 / #1F1A17 / #6A605A / #EF5A24 / #FBE6DB Ink 16.3:1 · muted 5.8:1. Energy Orange at full value takes dark text #1F1A17 on the button (5.1:1); if you need white button text, drop the accent to #C2410C (5.2:1). Reserve orange for add-to-cart and price urgency.

6. Luxury, jewellery, fine dining

#1E3A2C / #EDEFE9 / #A9B3A6 / #EBB13C / #2A4A39 Ink 10.7:1 · muted 5.7:1 · deep green text on the maize accent, 6.4:1. Deep Green as the new black, with maize as metal. Never white on maize.

7. Fintech and banking

#F5F6F8 / #0E1726 / #5C6678 / #1E3FCB / #E3E7F6 Ink 16.6:1 · muted 5.4:1 · white on accent 8.0:1. The one category where a cooler neutral is still correct — it reads as precision. Positive #137A4B, critical #B3261E, and never rely on hue alone for a balance.

8. Education and courses

#FAF7F1 / #1B1A17 / #5F5B52 / #2F5FD0 / #E2E8F8 Ink 16.3:1 · muted 6.3:1 · white on accent 5.7:1. Warm paper surface plus a friendly blue. Keep progress indicators in the accent so learners read progress as the primary signal.

9. Wellness, yoga, fitness

#F2F0EA / #1D231C / #5B6157 / #3F686D / #E0E7E7 Ink 14.1:1 · muted 5.6:1 · white on accent 6.2:1. Patina blue-green rather than sage — sage has become the default and now reads as a template. Avoid pure white; the whole point is a softer surface.

10. Food, restaurants, cafés

#FAF5EC / #231A12 / #6B5C4A / #A6361F / #F4E0D8 Ink 15.8:1 · muted 5.9:1 · white on accent 6.6:1. Appetite colours are warm and slightly dirty, not neon. Let photography carry the saturation and keep the interface recessive.

#F6F5F2 / #141A1F / #5A6369 / #2F4A6B / #E2E7EE Ink 16.1:1 · muted 5.6:1 · white on accent 9.1:1. Restrained slate blue. In this category the images are the design; the palette's job is to disappear behind them.

#F4F2F7 / #1B1726 / #645E74 / #C2185B / #F7E1EC Ink 15.8:1 · muted 5.6:1 · white on accent 5.9:1. Pop Pink at full value (#E24A93) is a display colour only — use it for large type and graphics, and the darker #C2185B wherever text sits on it.

14. Legal, accounting, consulting

#F6F5F1 / #14181C / #565C62 / #4A5D3A / #E4E8DE Ink 16.4:1 · muted 6.2:1 · white on accent 7.2:1. A muted olive instead of the expected navy. Same authority, immediately more memorable in a category where everyone looks identical.

#F7F4EE / #1A211A / #5A6158 / #4E7238 / #E4EADF Ink 15.0:1 · muted 5.8:1 · white on accent 5.5:1. Meadowland Green darkened enough to carry white text. Keep donation CTAs in the accent and nothing else, or the ask disappears.

16. Media, publishing, editorial

#F0EEE9 / #111111 / #575450 / #B33A2B / #F2DFD9 Ink 16.3:1 · muted 6.5:1 · white on accent 5.9:1. Cloud Dancer as the literal page. True black ink, one editorial red for links, section marks, and pull quotes.

17. Gaming and entertainment

#12101A / #EDEAF5 / #918BA6 / #6B45F0 / #241E3D Ink 15.9:1 · muted 5.8:1 · white on accent 5.6:1. Hued dark surface, never #000. If you want the neon look, add a second accent used only at 1–2px: borders, glows, focus rings.

18. Kids, family, parenting

#FFF9EF / #26201A / #6B6057 / #26688F / #DCE8EF Ink 15.4:1 · muted 5.8:1 · white on accent 6.1:1. Bright colours belong in illustration, not in the interface chrome. Parents are the ones reading the text, so contrast is set for adults.

Cross-category rules

  • Positive / critical defaults that pass on any light surface above: positive #137A4B (white text 4.9:1), critical #B3261E (white text 6.9:1).
  • Dark-mode conversion: lift the accent 10–15% in lightness, set the surface to a hued dark (#12151C, #1E3A2C, #12101A), and re-check every pair. A palette that passes in light mode does not automatically pass in dark.
  • If two categories overlap (a clinic that sells products, an agency that runs courses), take the surface and ink from the more conservative category and the accent from the more commercial one.

ไทป์ไทย

Thai and bilingual (TH/EN) websites

Read this whenever the site has any Thai content. Thai is not English with different glyphs — it breaks four assumptions baked into most Latin-first design systems, and a palette that passes in English can fail in Thai.

1. Why colour choices change

Thai glyphs stack. Vowels and tone marks sit above and below the baseline, so a line of Thai text has more ink and more vertical complexity than Latin at the same size. Consequences for the palette:

  • Muted text needs more contrast in Thai. A #5B6072 on #F0EEE9 that reads fine in English gets fuzzy in Thai at 14px. For Thai body copy target 5.5:1 minimum rather than 4.5:1, or raise the size.
  • Thin weights collapse. Thai at weight 300 loses its tone marks on low-DPI screens. Body text should be 400 minimum, and small labels 500.
  • Low-chroma text on tinted surfaces is riskier. Keep accent-coloured body text out of Thai layouts entirely; accent belongs on buttons and links only.
  • Dark mode hurts Thai more. Light-on-dark blooms and the tone marks merge into the ascender. On dark surfaces, avoid weights above 600 for Thai and give it slightly more line-height than the English version.

2. Typeface selection

Two families of Thai type, and the choice signals a lot:

Style Reads as Use for Google Fonts
Looped (มีหัว) Official, trustworthy, readable Government, medical, legal, education, anything for readers over 40 Sarabun, Noto Sans Thai, IBM Plex Sans Thai Looped
Loopless (ไม่มีหัว) Modern, designed, urban Tech, fashion, F&B, creative, youth Anuphan, IBM Plex Sans Thai, LINE Seed Sans TH
Display Loud, promotional Headlines only, never body Kanit, Prompt, Chakra Petch, Bai Jamjuree

Rules: - Never set Thai body copy in Kanit or Prompt. They are display faces. This is the single most common mistake on Thai commercial sites. - Match optical weight across scripts. Latin 600 usually needs Thai 600, but some Thai faces run heavy — check side by side rather than trusting the number. - If the client is a hospital or clinic, default to Sarabun or Noto Sans Thai. Looped type carries institutional credibility that loopless does not.

3. Layout rules

  • Line-height: Thai needs 1.75–1.9. Latin at 1.5 looks generous; the same value clips Thai tone marks against the line above.
  • Letter-spacing must be 0 or normal. Tracking splits Thai character clusters and makes words unreadable. Any letter-spacing you set for Latin headings has to be reset under :lang(th).
  • No text-transform: uppercase. Thai has no case; the rule does nothing to Thai and creates an inconsistent bilingual system.
  • Don't justify text. Thai has no spaces between words, so browsers break lines at cluster boundaries. Justified Thai produces huge random gaps.
  • Thai runs 15–25% longer than the same English copy. Buttons, nav items, and card headings need to flex, not be fixed-width. Design the Thai version first if you can — fitting English into a Thai-sized box always works, the reverse rarely does.
  • Bump Thai up 1–1.5px. Thai at 16px reads smaller than Latin at 16px.

4. Building the two versions

Prefer separate URLs (/th/ and /en/) over a JavaScript toggle if search traffic matters. Google needs a crawlable URL per language, and Thai-language search is where most local demand sits. A toggle is fine for a prototype or an app-like product page.

Either way:

  • Set lang="th" or lang="en" on <html> — it changes font fallback and line breaking, not just semantics.
  • Add hreflang pairs for both versions plus x-default.
  • Keep the language switcher in the header, always visible, labelled in the target language (English / ไทย), never as a flag. Flags mean countries, not languages.
  • Translate the whole page or none of it. Half-translated pages read as abandoned and cost more trust than an English-only page would.
  • Numbers, prices, and dates: Thai sites use Arabic numerals and the Gregorian calendar for commerce. Thai numerals and the Buddhist era belong in official and legal contexts — match what the client's competitors do.

5. CSS pattern

:root { --leading-latin: 1.55; --leading-thai: 1.8; }

body { font-family: 'Inter Tight', sans-serif; line-height: var(--leading-latin); }

:lang(th), html[lang="th"] body {
  font-family: 'IBM Plex Sans Thai', 'Noto Sans Thai', sans-serif;
  line-height: var(--leading-thai);
  letter-spacing: 0;              /* mandatory reset */
  font-size: 1.06em;              /* optical size match */
}
html[lang="th"] h1,
html[lang="th"] h2 {
  font-family: 'Anuphan', sans-serif;
  letter-spacing: 0;              /* kills the Latin display tracking */
  line-height: 1.25;              /* tight Latin leading clips tone marks */
}
html[lang="th"] .label { text-transform: none; }

Always load the Thai family — do not rely on the system fallback. Thai system fonts differ so much between iOS, Android and Windows that an unloaded fallback changes the entire feel of the page.

Use this skill whenever the user mentions colour, color, palette, hex codes, brand colours, "what colour should I use", theming, dark mode, design tokens, CSS variables, a rebrand, a moodboard, or asks you to design or restyle any website, landing page, dashboard, deck, or social template — even if they don't say the word "palette". Also use it when reviewing an existing design for contrast, accessibility, or brand consistency.

Colour Skill

Turn a vague brief ("make it look modern", "something calm for a clinic") into a named, documented colour system with real hex values, WCAG contrast evidence, and usage rules a developer or Canva designer can follow without asking follow-ups.

The 60-second version

  1. Pin the brief: category, audience, region, light/dark, what must convert.
  2. Pick the neutral first, accent second. The neutral is 80–90% of the pixels and decides whether the site feels 2021 or 2027. Never start with the accent.
  3. Build exactly 12 tokens (see Token set below). Not 40. Not 5.
  4. Prove contrast before shipping. Every text/background pair gets a ratio.
  5. Ship in three formats: a token table, a CSS :root block, and a plain-language usage rule per token.

Never hand back a loose list of pretty hex codes. That is a moodboard, not a system.

Step 1 — Pin the brief

Ask only what you can't infer. Minimum you need:

Input Why it matters
Website category Decides the palette family — see references/category-palettes.md
Audience age / market Older or medical audiences need higher contrast and fewer hues
Region Colour meaning is not universal — see Regional notes below
Light, dark, or both Dark mode is not "invert the hex", it needs its own ramp
Primary conversion action The accent exists to serve this and nothing else
Existing brand assets Logo colour is a constraint, not a suggestion

If the user has a logo or existing site, sample from it and work around it rather than proposing a replacement, unless they asked for a rebrand.

Step 2 — Choose the direction

Read references/trend-2027.md for the forecast detail and the exact approximate hex values. The short version of where colour is heading:

  • Neutrals carry the design now. Pantone's 2026 Colour of the Year is Cloud Dancer, a soft white. Pure #FFFFFF reads as cheap and clinical; warm or mineral off-whites (#F0EEE9, #F8F5F1) read as considered.
  • One saturated accent, used rarely. WGSN x Coloro's 2027 Colour of the Year is Luminous Blue, a cobalt that deliberately bridges "natural" and "digital".
  • Earth tones stay, but as neutrals, not as the whole palette. Clay, russet, and mineral beige are the supporting cast for 2027, not the star.
  • Dark mode becomes designed, not inverted. Deep Green is being positioned as the new black — a dark surface with a hue in it.

Pick one dominant direction plus one accent direction. Two dominant directions is how palettes fall apart.

Step 3 — The token set

Always produce exactly these twelve. Name them by role, never by colour ("accent", not "blue") so the system survives a rebrand.

surface          the main canvas
surface-raised   cards, panels, sidebars
surface-sunken   input fields, code blocks, wells
border           hairlines and dividers
ink              body text
ink-muted        captions, labels, secondary text
ink-inverse      text sitting on accent or on dark surfaces
accent           the single brand colour — CTAs, links, active states
accent-hover     one step darker or lighter, for interaction feedback
accent-quiet     8–12% tint of accent, for selected rows and badges
positive         success, confirmation, in-stock
critical         errors, destructive actions, expiry

Rules that go with them:

  • The accent appears on less than 10% of any screen. If it's everywhere, nothing is emphasised.
  • positive and critical are never the accent, even if the brand is green or red.
  • Never carry meaning in hue alone — pair every status colour with an icon or label.
  • Gradients are for atmosphere on large surfaces only, never behind body text.

Step 4 — Prove contrast

Before presenting anything, compute and state the ratio for: ink on surface, ink-muted on surface, ink-inverse on accent, and accent on surface.

Targets: 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for text 24px+ or bold 19px+, and 3:1 for button edges, icons, and focus rings against their background.

Contrast ratio = (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05) where L is relative luminance. If computing by hand is slow, write a five-line script — don't estimate.

If a trend colour fails, keep the hue and change the lightness, don't abandon the direction. Luminous Blue at its true value fails as body text on white; it passes comfortably as a button fill with white text.

Step 5 — Deliver

Three blocks, in this order:

  1. Token table — token, hex, role, contrast note.
  2. CSS — a :root block, plus a [data-theme="dark"] block if dark mode was requested. Use the same token names in both.
  3. Usage rules — one plain sentence per token, written for whoever is actually building it. If the client works in Canva rather than code, give the same values as a Brand Kit list with "use this for headings / backgrounds / buttons" phrasing instead of CSS.

Add a one-line rationale tying the palette to the brief. Not a paragraph of colour-psychology filler — one line, e.g. "Mineral neutral base keeps a treatment-heavy page calm; the cobalt is reserved entirely for booking CTAs."

Regional notes

  • Any Thai content: stop and read references/thai-typography.md before setting type or finalising muted-text values. Thai needs ~5.5:1 for body copy, letter-spacing: 0, and line-height around 1.8.
  • Thailand / SEA: high-saturation palettes perform well in social and LINE creative but the same saturation on a landing page reduces perceived credibility for medical and financial services. Split the system: a calm web palette and a louder social palette that share the same accent.
  • Medical and clinic sites (Thailand): regulators respond badly to visuals that imply guaranteed outcomes. Avoid gold/"luxury" gradients and dramatic before/after colour grading that exaggerates results — the copy rules and the colour choices get read together.
  • Purple reads as innovation in Western B2B, but as mourning in parts of Latin America and Southern Europe. White carries funeral associations in parts of East Asia — pair it with warmth if the market is Chinese or Korean.

Common failure modes

Symptom Fix
"It looks AI-generated" You used cream #F4F1EA + terracotta #D97757 + a high-contrast serif. Change the neutral's temperature.
Palette looks muddy Too many mid-tones. Force distance: very light surface, very dark ink, one saturated accent.
CTA doesn't stand out The accent is being used decoratively elsewhere. Remove it from everything but actions.
Dark mode looks dirty You inverted rather than rebuilt. Dark surfaces need a hue (not #000), and the accent usually needs lifting 10–15% in lightness.
Client keeps asking for changes You showed colours without roles. Re-present as a token table with usage rules.

Color Pattern

Turn a palette into seamless tiles that repeat with no visible seam, in colourways that stay on-brand.

Use scripts/patterns.py — do not hand-write SVG pattern maths. Tiling errors are invisible in a single tile and obvious once repeated, and the script already handles edge wrapping.

Quick start

python3 scripts/patterns.py --list
python3 scripts/patterns.py \
  --palette "#F0EEE9,#1E3FCB,#EBB13C,#8B4A33" \
  --out ./out --title "Client pattern set"

The first colour is always the background; every colour after it is a motif colour. Output is one .svg per pattern plus index.html, a contact sheet showing each tile actually repeating.

Flags: --only dots,waves,terrazzo to pick patterns, --seed 42 to reshuffle the randomised ones (scatter, terrazzo, arcs), --out for the folder.

The workflow

  1. Get the palette. If the user has one, use it. If not, build one first — that is the colour-skill's job, not this one — it builds the twelve-token system and proves contrast. Do not invent colours here. Feed the finished palette in as --palette "surface,accent,…", surface first.
  2. Decide the pattern's job before picking a style. See the table below.
  3. Generate a set, not a single tile. Users pick from options; generating one and defending it wastes a round trip.
  4. Show the contact sheet, not the tiles. A tile viewed alone tells you nothing about whether it repeats well.
  5. Cut to a shortlist of three, with a sentence on where each one belongs.

Choosing a pattern for the job

The pattern must… Use Avoid
Sit behind body text grid, dots, crosses at low contrast terrazzo, stripes, checker
Fill a hero or section band waves, scallop, arcs, chevron grid (reads as a spreadsheet)
Read as premium or crafted herringbone, scallop, arcs checker, halftone-style dots
Read as playful terrazzo, scatter, triangles, dots grid, stripes
Work on packaging or print stripes, herringbone, terrazzo anything with hairlines under 1pt
Work as a tiny avatar or favicon checker, triangles, chevron scatter, terrazzo, grid

Contrast rules — the opposite of interface rules

A palette that passes WCAG makes a bad background pattern. The rule inverts:

  • Behind text: keep motif and background within ~1.3:1. Tint the motif, or drop it to 8–15% opacity. If you can read the pattern, you cannot read the text.
  • As a feature: go to full strength, but then nothing else sits on top of it.
  • Never put a pattern behind body copy in dark mode unless the motif is within a few percent of the surface lightness — light-on-dark blooms.
  • Scale is contrast. The same tile at 40px reads as texture and at 300px reads as graphic. Test at the size it will actually be used.

Colourways

A pattern sells or ships as a set, not a single file. Standard set of four from one palette:

  1. Full — background plus every motif colour.
  2. Duotone — background plus the accent only.
  3. Tonal — background plus one motif colour at 15% opacity. This is the one that actually gets used behind text.
  4. Inverted — darkest colour as background, lightest as motif.

Generate each by reordering the --palette argument rather than editing SVG.

Export

SVG is the master. It scales without loss and the colours stay editable — keep it as the working file.

For raster delivery, render the tile at the pixel size it will repeat at, not larger. A tile exported at 2000px and displayed at 100px looks soft.

Use Tile export Notes
Web background 200–600px PNG, or the SVG itself SVG is smaller for flat-colour patterns
Social template 1080×1080 filled, not the bare tile Build the repeat into the canvas
Print / packaging SVG or 300dpi at final size Confirm CMYK conversion of the accent
Sold as a product SVG + PNG at 3000×3000 + a JPG preview Include a colourway sheet in the listing

Rendering to PNG: cairosvg tile.svg -o tile.png --output-width 600

Rules that prevent the two common failures

Seams. A motif that crosses the tile edge must reappear on the opposite edge. The script does this by drawing every motif nine times at offsets of ±W and ±H; the SVG viewBox clips the overflow. Any motif spacing must divide the tile size exactly — 200 ÷ 40 works, 200 ÷ 14 does not. See references/tiling.md for the full maths and the failure cases.

Visible repeat. Seamless is not the same as invisible. Random placement leaves voids, and the eye locks onto a repeating void faster than onto a repeating motif. Use a jittered grid instead of pure random, and never use a one-directional size or colour ramp inside a tile — it always bands.

Always verify by rendering the tile repeated at least 2×2 and looking at it. Do not ship a tile you have only seen once.

Reference files

  • references/tiling.md — seamless tiling maths, the nine-copy method, which spacings are legal, and how to debug a seam.
  • scripts/patterns.py — the generator. Read --list output before promising a user a pattern style that does not exist yet; adding one is about 10 lines.

Seamless tiling — the maths and the failure cases

The nine-copy method

A tile is seamless when translating it by exactly one tile width or height maps the artwork onto itself. The general way to guarantee that, without clever per-pattern geometry, is to draw every motif nine times:

for dx in (-W, 0, +W):
    for dy in (-H, 0, +H):
        <g transform="translate(dx,dy)"> …motifs… </g>

An SVG root element clips to its viewBox, so the eight extra copies are invisible except where they poke into the tile from outside — which is precisely the content that must appear there for the edges to meet.

Cost is nine times the node count. For flat-colour patterns this is irrelevant. For anything with hundreds of motifs, generate the tile and then flatten it.

Which spacings are legal

If a motif repeats every p units, then W must be an exact multiple of p. This is the single most common cause of a seam.

  • W = 200, p = 40 → 5 repeats. Legal.
  • W = 200, p = 14 → 14.28 repeats. Seam.
  • W = 200, p = 25 → 8 repeats. Legal.

For half-drop patterns (every other row offset by half a step, like scallop or herringbone), the number of rows must additionally be even, or the offset lands on the wrong phase at the seam.

For 45° diagonals, do not rotate a set of vertical bars — a rotation about the tile centre does not commute with the tile translation. Draw lines of slope 1 instead (y = x + c), spaced by a divisor of W, and require W == H. Then a shift of one tile in either axis maps the line set onto itself.

Colour cycles must match geometry cycles

Geometry can tile perfectly while colour does not. If the tile contains n repeats of a motif and the palette has m motif colours, the colour index must be periodic with n, not with m:

# wrong — colour(i) != colour(i + n) unless m divides n
fill = motif[i % m]

# right
fill = motif[(i % n) % m]

Symptom: the shapes line up across the seam but the colours jump.

Ramps always band

Any property that varies monotonically across the tile — dot size growing top to bottom, opacity fading left to right, a colour gradient — cannot meet itself at the seam. The tile is technically seamless only if the ramp wraps, and a wrapped ramp reads as visible banding when repeated.

If you need a fade, apply it as a separate gradient layer over a uniform tile. Do not build it into the tile.

Debugging a seam

  1. Render the tile to PNG at its natural size.
  2. Paste it into a 2×2 grid.
  3. Look at it. This catches more than any metric.

Automated checks are unreliable here: comparing the wrap edge against interior pixel differences flags every pattern whose legitimate feature boundary happens to fall on the tile edge — a checkerboard fails a naive seam test while being perfectly seamless. Use the metric to shortlist, then use your eyes to decide.

If a seam is real, it is almost always one of:

Symptom Cause
Hard line at one edge only A motif drawn at both 0 and W, doubling the stroke
Shapes cut in half at the edge Missing the nine-copy wrap
Shapes align, colours jump Colour cycle does not divide the geometry cycle
Regular grid of gaps in a random pattern Pure random placement leaving voids
Bands across the repeat A ramp built into the tile
